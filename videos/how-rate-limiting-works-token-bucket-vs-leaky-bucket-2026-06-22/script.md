You have seen this message. "You're doing that too much. Try again in a minute." Behind that polite little refusal sits a gate, quietly counting how fast you knock. It feels like a minor annoyance. But take that gate away, and a single attacker can throw three hundred and ninety-eight million requests a second at you.

<!-- BODY -->
That number is real. In 2023, Google absorbed a flood that peaked right there, around three hundred and ninety-eight million requests every second, more than seven times the worst it had ever seen. It came from an attack called Rapid Reset, and Cloudflare and Amazon were hit in the same wave. No server farm on earth serves traffic that fast. The only thing standing between a service and that kind of flood is something that can say "enough" before the request ever reaches the real machinery.

And the threat isn't always an attacker. Sometimes a system strangles itself. When a service starts to struggle, its own clients panic and retry, and those retries pile onto the thing that's already drowning. Engineers call it a retry storm. In 2015, one of these took Amazon's DynamoDB down for about five hours, with more than half of all requests failing, dragging Netflix and Reddit dark with it. <!-- TTS: DynamoDB = "Dynamo D-B" --> The math is merciless. Five services deep, each retrying three times, and the database at the bottom sees two hundred and forty-three times the original load. There is even a name for the end state, where a system is so buried in retries it can no longer move at all. Congestive collapse. Amazon used that exact phrase in October 2025, after a fourteen-hour outage that took down more than a hundred and forty of its own services.

So every serious service plants a gate at the very edge, before the login, before the business logic, before the database. The gate asks one question, over and over, millions of times a second. Is this request allowed, right now? There are two classic ways to answer it. And both of them are named after a bucket.

Start with the token bucket. Picture a bucket that holds ten tokens, no more — that ceiling is the whole trick. A tap drips two fresh tokens in every second, but once the bucket is full at ten, the extra drips just spill over the rim and vanish. Every request you make pulls one token out and spends it. If there's a token to spend, you're through, instantly. If the bucket is empty, the gate throws an error called 429, Too Many Requests, and tells you to come back once the tap has dripped a new one. The clever part is what sitting still buys you. Stay quiet for five seconds and the bucket refills to ten, so now you can fire ten requests back to back — a burst — before settling into the steady two a second the tap allows. There are exactly two knobs, and they move apart. How fast the tap drips, which is your sustained rate. And how deep the bucket is, which is your burst. This is the gate Stripe runs on its API, capped near a hundred requests a second. It's the gate inside Amazon's API Gateway, ten thousand a second, with five thousand held in the bucket for a spike. The token bucket forgives a burst. It just won't let you sustain one.

Now the leaky bucket. There are actually two of them. The first is a literal bucket with a hole in the bottom. Requests pour in from the top, and they drain out the hole at one fixed, unchanging rate. Throw a hundred requests at it in a single millisecond, and it doesn't flinch. They queue up inside and leave one at a time, smoothed into a perfectly even stream. This is how the web server Nginx paces traffic. <!-- TTS: Nginx = "engine X" --> One request every hundred milliseconds, the rest waiting politely in line, until the bucket overflows and the extras spill out, rejected.

The second leaky bucket is stranger. It never holds a request at all. The first bucket kept your requests waiting inside; this one keeps nothing but a number. Picture a bouncer at the door with a clicker. Every request that arrives is sent straight to the server, instantly, and the bouncer thumbs the counter up by one. That counter ticks back down on its own, two a second, the gate quietly forgetting. The cap is ten. While the counter sits under ten, every request is waved through the moment it arrives. But the instant one more would push it past ten, that request is turned away — a 429, dropped on the spot, never parked in a line. Nobody waits here. Idle for a second and the counter leaks from ten to eight, and the door opens for two more.

So line them up. The first leaky bucket, the queue, does exactly what the name promises — no bursts, just a steady trickle, every request filed into one even line. But the second one, the counter, quietly breaks that promise. Let it sit idle until the counter drains to zero, then throw ten requests at it in the same instant. Every one is under the cap, so every one is waved straight through, immediately. That's a burst — the very thing a leaky bucket is supposed to forbid. Because under the mask, the counter was never really a leaky bucket. It's the token bucket, counting backwards. One ticks down from ten as you spend it; the other ticks up to ten as you fill it; feed them the same cap and the same rate, and they make the identical call on every request.

So forget the names. Here's the only choice that matters. When a clump of requests lands at once, you either let them through, or you spread them into a slow, even line. The token bucket lets the burst through — perfect for a public API, where a user opening ten tabs at once is just honest traffic. The leaky-bucket queue refuses the burst and drips every request out at one steady rate — perfect for a fragile system downstream that needs calm, even flow. One allows a burst. The other forces a trickle. That's the whole decision.

The two buckets aren't the only answers to rate limiting or throttling. They're just the ones that keep your sustained rate and your burst as two separate dials. The simplest gate of all is a fixed window — a fixed budget over a fixed stretch of time, reset all at once when the stretch is up. Your Claude Code cap works exactly this way. The clock starts on your first message and runs five hours; spend your whole allowance inside it and you're locked out, watching a timer count down — resets in three hours, eighteen minutes — until it flips back to full and a fresh window opens. Cheap to run, with one blind spot: the boundary. Wait for the tail end of your window, burn the whole allowance, then send one message the instant it resets to open a new window and burn it all again. Two full budgets back to back — and the gate never flinches, because it files them as two separate windows and can't see across the line between them.

The fix is a rolling window — the kind Cloudflare reaches for. Forget a counter that resets on a schedule. Instead, the gate just watches the clock. Say it's two in the afternoon: it counts every request you've made in the last five hours — from nine that morning up to right now — and checks that against your limit. Under the line, you're in. Over it, you wait. The clever part is that nothing ever resets. The window just slides. An hour later, at three, it's counting from ten that morning to three. The five-hour window crawls forward with the clock, so the requests you made five hours ago quietly fall off the back and free up that much room again. No single reset to wait for, no boundary to game — the seam is gone, because there's no line left to stand on either side of.

And here's the loop that ties it all off. Remember the retry storm, the clients that hammered a service until it drowned? In 2016, Amazon taught its own software to stop doing exactly that, by putting a token bucket on its retries. Free to retry while there are tokens, and the moment they run out, it stops retrying instead of piling on. The same bucket that throttles you at the gate is the bucket a system uses to keep from strangling itself.
