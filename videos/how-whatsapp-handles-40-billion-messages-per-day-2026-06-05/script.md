Forty billion outbound messages a day. More than two million live connections on a single server. Around thirty-two engineers running the whole thing. Back in 2014, the first and last of those numbers described WhatsApp — and together, they should not have been possible.

<!-- BODY -->
So how does so little run so much? Let's follow one message, from the moment your thumb hits send.

Your phone is not making a fresh request for every text. It holds one persistent TCP connection — a single long-lived socket, opened once, kept open. That socket is the first surprise. It doesn't reconnect per message; it stays alive, waiting, for hours. But a warm pipe has a price. An open socket is memory and a file handle the server must hold, even when nothing is being said. Multiply that by hundreds of millions of mostly-idle phones, and just keeping the connections alive becomes the hard part. That is the problem the next choice had to solve.

Your thumb hits send. Before it even leaves, the message is squeezed down. WhatsApp speaks a compact binary protocol called FunXMPP, trading verbose tags for single-byte tokens to spare your data and your battery. <!-- TTS: FunXMPP = "fun X-M-P-P" --> The slimmed-down message travels up that open socket to a chat server, and lands inside a process that exists only for you. This is the heart of the design, called the actor model. The backend was built almost entirely in Erlang, a language from the telecom world. There, each connection becomes its own lightweight process on the BEAM virtual machine. Not a thread. Not a request handler. A tiny isolated worker, one per phone, alive for as long as your socket is.

That isolation is everything. When one process crashes, it dies and a supervisor respawns it, instead of taking the box down with it. Erlang engineers call this "let it crash." A failure stays contained to one user, which is partly how so few people could operate something this large. None of this comes free. Erlang is a niche language, with a hiring pool to match, and it was never built for raw number-crunching speed. What it was built for is holding millions of things at once without falling over. WhatsApp decided that was the trade worth making.

Now the message needs a destination. Your process asks one question — where does the recipient live right now? The answer sits in Mnesia, Erlang's in-memory distributed database, holding the routing table that maps each user to their server. Around two terabytes of it, kept in RAM. That choice matters at fan-out time. An in-memory lookup is fast enough to run on every single message, exactly what routing tens of billions of them demands. Memory, though, is the expensive seat in the house. Two terabytes of RAM is a real bill, and an in-memory store has to be replicated carefully — lose a node, and you lose state. They traded durability and cost for raw speed. At fan-out time, speed wins.

But one message isn't always headed to one person. Send it to a group, and your phone still uploads exactly once — a single copy, tagged with a group ID. The expansion happens on the server. A chat process takes that one message and fans it out, looking up every member and queuing a copy for each. This is server-side fan-out. To keep that work from piling onto one box, group and presence routing was partitioned thirty-two ways across Erlang's distributed process groups. One upload becomes hundreds of deliveries, spread across the cluster. And this is why the numbers never quite add up. A "sent" count and a "delivered" count drift apart, because every group message multiplies on the way out. On the record day, the second of April, 2014, twenty billion messages users sent fanned out into forty-four billion delivered — sixty-four billion in total. The gap is fan-out, made visible. And it cuts both ways. Fan-out is generous to your phone and brutal to the server — one upload becomes hundreds of deliveries, so the cost scales with the size of the group, not with how many people are typing. Big groups are quietly one of the most expensive things the system does.

Either way, the message still has to reach a person. Mnesia returns a server and a session. Your process hands the message to the recipient's process, and if they're online, it flows straight down their open socket. One hop in, one hop out. The servers never let two phones talk directly. Everything routes through the middle, a pattern called store-and-forward.

But the recipient isn't always there. Phones die, tunnels swallow signal, people sleep. When the destination process doesn't exist, the message drops into an encrypted offline queue, and simply waits. The server holds it, and holds it, for up to thirty days. When that phone reconnects, its process respawns and pulls the queue, hours after the trip began. But come back after thirty days, and it's already gone — the server drops the message, and that first grey tick never finds its second. Delivered or expired, the server forgets it — a relay, not a mailbox.

This is where the ticks come in. The two grey ticks you watch turn blue are not decoration; they're the message reporting home. One grey tick means the server received it. Two grey means it reached the recipient's device, the moment they reconnect and drain the queue. Two blue means it was read. The ticks are the architecture, leaking through to your screen.

The ticks aren't the only thing that socket carries. So do the small signals beside them — the "typing" dots, the "last seen" timestamp, the green presence dot. But these ride their own lightweight notification stream, deliberately kept off the message path. They are server-tracked session state, not phone-to-phone. The heavy lifting of delivery never waits on whether someone is online — two jobs, one pipe, kept apart on purpose.

So far, the server has known everything. Where you live, when you reconnect, which queue holds your backlog. The encryption beat is where that knowing stops. It didn't arrive all at once. On the eighteenth of November, 2014, WhatsApp partnered with Open Whisper Systems — Moxie Marlinspike and Trevor Perrin, the people behind the Signal Protocol. Then on the fifth of April, 2016, end-to-end encryption switched on by default for over a billion users — the largest such deployment at the time. The mechanism comes in two moves. First, a prekey handshake called X3DH lets two people agree on a shared key even when one of them is offline. <!-- TTS: X3DH = "X-three-D-H" --> Their prekeys wait on the server, ready. Then the Double Ratchet takes over, deriving a fresh key for every single message. That gives you forward secrecy. Steal one key, and you still can't read the messages before it or after it. Here is the consequence. Everything we just followed — the routing, the offline queue, the group fan-out — now moves ciphertext the server cannot read. It still knows where to send the envelope. It never learns what's inside. A dumb, fast, blind pipe — which is exactly what you want it to be. But blindness has a bill. A server that can't read your messages can't search them for you, can't scan them for spam or abuse, and can't quietly rebuild your history on a new device. Every convenience that needs to read your content now has to happen on your phone — or not at all.

But that sealed envelope assumes one key per person. And people don't live on one device anymore. They want WhatsApp open on a phone and a laptop at once, and end-to-end encryption was never built for that. In 2021, Meta's engineers solved it with a quiet change. Each device gets its own identity key — your phone, your laptop, each a separate cryptographic identity. The server keeps a directory that maps your account to every device you own. When someone messages you, their phone looks up that directory and encrypts the message separately for each of your devices. This is called client-side fan-out — the sealing happens on the sender's phone, once per recipient device. The server still never holds a key. It holds a list of where the keys live, and nothing more. A directory, not a keyholder.

And it quietly reshapes who does the work. When you link that laptop, your history doesn't come down from the server — the server has nothing readable to send. Your phone does it. It encrypts a slice of your recent chats and hands them straight to the new device, with the key wrapped in its own end-to-end message. You're not downloading your past; you're uploading it, phone to laptop. The bill comes in three parts. The sender now seals every message several times over, once per device. The server forwards several copies where it used to forward one. And if your phone is offline the moment you link, there's no history to inherit. They traded a thin, simple mirror for true independence — and paid for it in duplication.

And all of this ran on metal you can describe. Not Linux — FreeBSD, chosen for a mature TCP stack and a kernel you could tune deep. <!-- TTS: FreeBSD = "free B-S-D" --> Both Koum and Reed came out of a FreeBSD-heavy Yahoo, so the choice was instinct as much as design. Buy big bare-metal boxes and tune them hard, instead of spraying many small nodes. Compute boxes ran dual ten-core Intel Xeon chips, sixty-four gigabytes of RAM, half a terabyte on the database machines. All of it sat on IBM SoftLayer bare metal — seven hundred-plus servers across two datacenters, for roughly two million dollars a month. And the team didn't just rent the hardware; they reached into it. They patched the BEAM runtime and the FreeBSD kernel both, hunting down lock contention. The metal was theirs. Owning it cut both ways. Bare-metal boxes are cheap and fast at a steady scale, but they don't appear on demand the way cloud instances do. You carry the capacity, you carry the failures, and you tune the kernel yourself. They chose control over elasticity, then had to live inside that choice.

And here is the architecture's quietest trick. Erlang can load new code into a running system. Fixes and features ship without a restart, the processes never stop, and the code beneath them changes mid-flight while they keep handling messages. That is the near-zero-downtime claim, and it mostly holds. But resilient is not the same as invincible. On the twenty-second of February, 2014 — three days after Facebook announced the nineteen-billion-dollar deal — WhatsApp went dark for about two hundred and ten minutes. Roughly three and a half hours. WhatsApp blamed a server problem, not the deal itself. The architecture absorbs failure gracefully; it does not abolish it.

So that's one message: socket, process, lookup, handoff, queue, ticks, envelope. Now multiply it. A single chat server doesn't hold one of these connections — it holds millions. Stack those boxes up and watch the line climb. Back in 2011, one server already held around a million live connections, with CPU and memory to spare. By 2012, at an Erlang Factory talk, engineer Rick Reed pushed a single box past two million. In production they ran each near a million on purpose, leaving headroom for failover and spikes. The volume climbed in step. Ten billion messages a day by August 2012. Around eighteen billion on New Year's Eve, 2012. Then nineteen billion in and forty billion out, every day, by the 2014 talk. And that one record day in April, sixty-four billion. The curve never flattened. By 2020, WhatsApp was carrying roughly a hundred billion a day. And the fleet that hauled the 2014 numbers was almost embarrassingly small. Around five hundred and fifty servers. Over eleven thousand CPU cores. A hundred and forty-seven million phones connected at once, at peak. Simple parts, repeated until the totals stop feeling real.

That's the architecture. One socket per phone, one process per socket, one in-RAM lookup per message, and supervisors that respawn whatever falls over. Which leaves one question hanging over all of it. Who was actually running this? The answer is the part that doesn't sound real. WhatsApp was founded in 2009 on a deliberately lean, "do less" culture — no ads, no games, no gimmicks, just the message. The architecture is that culture in code: roughly fourteen million users for every engineer on the team. Every design choice we just followed — the per-user process, the supervisor that restarts what breaks, the in-RAM lookup, the blind pipe — was a choice that let a tiny team sleep at night. The leverage wasn't an accident. It was the whole point. And it wasn't free either. A team that small is a bus factor waiting to happen, and a product that does this little is a product that says no to almost everything. The leverage and the limits were the same decision.
