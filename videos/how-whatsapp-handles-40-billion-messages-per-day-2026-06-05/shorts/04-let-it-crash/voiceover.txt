WhatsApp's engineers wanted their code to crash.

On purpose. There's an actual engineering philosophy for it: let it crash.

Most systems are built to never fail. They wrap everything in safety nets. They try to recover from every error.

WhatsApp's backend does the opposite. And it works because of the language underneath it. Erlang, born in the telecom world.

Every phone that connects gets its own tiny, isolated process. It runs on the BEAM virtual machine. Not a thread. Just one featherweight worker per person. Millions of them, running at once.

So when one breaks, it doesn't try to heal. It just dies, alone. A supervisor notices. It spawns a fresh, clean copy. And the other million processes never even feel it.

None of it comes free, though. Erlang is a niche language, with a hiring pool to match. It was never built for raw number-crunching speed. What it was built for is holding millions of things at once, without falling over.

So what does a tiny team actually buy, by betting everything on a language almost nobody hires for? To find out, subscribe.
